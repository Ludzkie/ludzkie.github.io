Science 9 – Quarter 3: The Blueprint of Life (DNA, Mutations & Effects)

Lesson 1, Part 1: DNA’s Structure and Role

1.1 Discovery and the Watson-Crick Double Helix Model

  • Chromosomes as Genetic Material: Following Gregor Mendel’s principles of heredity, advances in biochemistry and molecular biology established that genetic material is located inside chromosomes[cite: 1].
  • Watson & Crick (April 25, 1953): American biologist James Watson and English physicist Francis Crick published "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid" in the journal Nature, describing DNA as a double helix and explaining how genetic information is stored in the nucleus and passed across generations[cite: 2].
  • Key Scientific Contributions: Their model integrated findings from multiple researchers, most notably the 1952 X-ray diffraction image (Photo 51) taken by Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling, as well as work by Maurice Wilkins[cite: 2, 3].
  • Recognition: Watson, Crick, and Wilkins received the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine[cite: 2]. Rosalind Franklin (1920–1958) passed away from ovarian cancer in 1958 before receiving full contemporaneous recognition for her pivotal X-ray crystallography work[cite: 3].
  • Double Helix Architecture: Two antiparallel DNA strands wind around each other like a twisted ladder:
    • Backbone (Sides): Composed of alternating sugar and phosphate groups[cite: 2].
    • Rungs (Steps): Composed of nitrogenous base pairs connected by hydrogen bonds[cite: 2].
  • Complementary Base Pairing Rules:
    • Adenine (A) pairs only with Thymine (T) via two hydrogen bonds[cite: 2].
    • Cytosine (C) pairs only with Guanine (G) via three hydrogen bonds[cite: 2].
  • Helix Dimensions: Measures 20 Å in diameter, 34 Å per complete turn, and features alternating major and minor grooves[cite: 2].

1.2 Nucleotides, DNA vs. RNA, and Chromosomes to Traits

  • Nucleic Acids: Organic compounds that store genetic information, act as physical carriers of inheritance, and direct protein synthesis[cite: 2].
  • Nucleotide (Basic Building Block): Both DNA and RNA are made of repeating units called nucleotides, each consisting of three parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base[cite: 3].
  • Classes of Nitrogenous Bases:
    • Purines (Double-ringed): Adenine (A) and Guanine (G)[cite: 3].
    • Pyrimidines (Single-ringed): Cytosine (C), Thymine (T), and Uracil (U)[cite: 3].
Feature DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) RNA (Ribonucleic Acid)
Strand Structure Double-stranded helix[cite: 3] Typically single-stranded[cite: 3]
Base Pairing with Adenine Thymine (T)[cite: 3] Uracil (U)[cite: 3]
Primary Cellular Role Long-term, stable blueprint for storing complex genetic instructions[cite: 3] Carries out instructions and performs diverse roles in protein synthesis[cite: 3, 7]
  • Chromosomes & Genes: Most human body cells contain 23 pairs of chromosomes (46 chromosomes total) and roughly 20,000 to 25,000 genes[cite: 4]. A gene is a specific DNA segment (hundreds to millions of bases long) that carries instructions for building proteins and determining traits[cite: 4, 5].
  • Genotype vs. Phenotype:
    • Genotype: The organism's complete set of genes inside the nucleus[cite: 5].
    • Phenotype: The observable traits produced when proteins are expressed (e.g., eye color, skin color, hair type)[cite: 5, 9].
  • Filipino Science Highlight: Filipino-American molecular biologist Fr. Nicanor Pier Giorgio Austriaco (DOST Balik Scientist and UST researcher) studies aging genes in yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and yeast-based oral vaccines, earning the 2024 NRCP Gabay Award for his 25IR-VD pandemic model[cite: 5].

1.3 Central Dogma, Replication, Transcription, and Translation

The central dogma of molecular genetics explains how hereditary information flows from DNA → messenger RNA (mRNA) → Protein[cite: 5, 16]:

  1. DNA Replication (Making Identical DNA Copies):
    • Occurs during the S (synthesis) stage of interphase prior to cell division so every new cell gets an exact DNA copy (G1 chromosomes have 1 chromatid; S phase synthesizes sister chromatids)[cite: 6].
    • Semiconservative Process: Each new daughter DNA molecule keeps one original parental strand and builds one new complementary strand[cite: 6].
    • Key Enzymes: DNA helicase breaks hydrogen bonds to unwind the double helix; DNA primase initiates nucleotide addition; DNA polymerase builds the complementary strand (A–T, C–G) and proofreads for errors; DNA ligase seals breaks and repairs damaged DNA[cite: 6].
  2. Transcription (DNA to mRNA):
    • Takes place inside the nucleus because DNA cannot leave the nucleus[cite: 7].
    • RNA polymerase unzips a gene segment and uses one DNA strand as a template to synthesize a complementary mRNA copy (matching U with A, and C with G)[cite: 7].
    • The completed mRNA strand exits the nucleus and enters the cytoplasm[cite: 7].
  3. Translation (mRNA to Protein):
    • Takes place in the cytoplasm as mRNA attaches to a ribosome[cite: 7, 8].
    • The ribosome reads mRNA in three-base sequences called codons (e.g., AUG is the start codon for methionine; UAA, UAG, and UGA are stop codons)[cite: 7, 8, 9].
    • Transfer RNA (tRNA) matches its three-base anticodon to the complementary mRNA codon and delivers the corresponding amino acid (e.g., mRNA codon GUA pairs with tRNA anticodon CAU to bring valine)[cite: 8].
    • Ribosomes link 20 types of amino acids together with peptide bonds to form polypeptide chains that fold into functional proteins[cite: 8, 9].
  • Bioinformatics: An interdisciplinary field combining molecular biology and computer science to store, organize, and analyze large biological and genome datasets[cite: 8].

Lesson 1, Part 2: Mutation – When DNA Changes

2.1 Types of Genetic Mutations

A mutation is a change in an organism's DNA sequence or chromosomes, occurring naturally during cell division or triggered by external factors[cite: 10, 16].

A. Mutations Based on Nucleotide Sequence (Gene Mutations)

  • Point Mutation (Microlesion): Addition or deletion of a single nucleotide pair[cite: 10].
  • Frameshift Mutation: Insertion or deletion of multiple nucleotides that shifts the codon reading frame[cite: 10].
  • Transverse Mutation: Replacement of a purine base with a pyrimidine base, or vice versa[cite: 10].
  • Substitution Mutation: One base pair is replaced by another (e.g., in the hemoglobin gene causing sickle cell anemia), or causes no phenotype change (silent mutation)[cite: 10].

B. Mutations Based on Chromosome Structure

Chromosomal Change What Happens to the Chromosome
Deletion[cite: 11] Loss of a DNA segment or whole chromosome segment[cite: 11].
Duplication[cite: 11] A DNA segment is copied twice in excess of the normal amount[cite: 11].
Inversion[cite: 11] A chromosome/DNA segment breaks off and flips in the opposite direction[cite: 11].
Translocation[cite: 11] A chromosome/DNA segment moves or transfers to another nonhomologous chromosome[cite: 11].

C. Mutations Based on Chromosome Number

  • Euploidy vs. Aneuploidy: Errors during cell division (meiosis or mitosis) can alter the whole chromosome set (euploidy) or result in one extra or missing chromosome (aneuploidy)[cite: 11].
  • Nondisjunction: The failure of homologous chromosomes to separate properly during cell division, leading to gametes with the wrong number of chromosomes[cite: 11].
  • Common Examples of Aneuploidy:
    • Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21): Three copies of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes total, 2n = 47), leading to distinct physical and intellectual developmental differences[cite: 11].
    • Turner Syndrome: Females with only one X chromosome (2n = 45; 44 autosomes + X)[cite: 11].
    • Klinefelter Syndrome: Males with an extra X chromosome (2n = 47; 44 autosomes + XXY)[cite: 11].
    • Metafemale (Trisomy X): Females with three X chromosomes (2n = 47; 44 autosomes + XXX)[cite: 11].

2.2 Causes of Mutation (Mutagens)

While some mutations happen spontaneously during replication, many are triggered by external environmental agents called mutagens[cite: 12]:

  • Radiation: High-energy ionizing radiation can break DNA strands or alter bases—including UV rays from sunlight (linked to skin cancer), X-rays (safe in small medical doses, harmful with prolonged/repeated exposure), and nuclear disaster radiation (e.g., Chernobyl and Fukushima)[cite: 12].
  • Chemicals: Everyday toxic substances that alter genes—including cigarette or vape smoke (over 60 known mutagens linked to lung cancer), agricultural pesticides and herbicides, and heavy metals (lead, arsenic, mercury, cadmium) from mining, industrial waste, or polluted water[cite: 12].
  • Infectious Agents: Viruses, bacteria, and parasites that interfere with host DNA or cause chronic inflammation—such as human papillomavirus (HPV) linked to cervical cancer and hepatitis B virus linked to liver cancer[cite: 12].

Lesson 1, Part 3: Effects of Mutation on Organisms and Populations

3.1 Beneficial, Harmful, and Neutral Mutations

  • Beneficial Mutations: Help organisms adapt better, survive, reproduce, or resist diseases, spreading through populations via natural selection[cite: 13]:
    • Lactose Tolerance: Allows adults to digest milk, providing a survival advantage in early farming communities[cite: 13].
    • CCR5-delta32 Mutation: Makes it harder for HIV to infect immune cells and offers resistance to atherosclerosis[cite: 13].
    • High-Altitude Adaptations: Mutations in populations living in the Andes Mountains and Tibetan Plateau boost oxygen-carrying capacity and regulate blood pressure in low-oxygen (hypoxic) environments[cite: 13].
  • Harmful Mutations: Damage specific genes and lead to genetic disorders[cite: 13]:
    • Heritable Genetic Disorders: Occur in germ line (sex) cells and are passed from parents to offspring[cite: 13].
    • Nonheritable Genetic Disorders: Occur in somatic (body) cells and cannot be inherited by offspring[cite: 13].
    • Disorders may come from a single gene mutation or be multifactorial/polygenic (multiple genes combined with lifestyle and environment)[cite: 14].
Genetic Disorder Genetic & Cellular Basis Key Symptoms & Effects
Tay-Sachs Disease[cite: 14] Autosomal recessive defect in an enzyme gene causing abnormal fatty buildup in nerve cells[cite: 14] Rare, fatal progressive nerve deterioration in brain and spinal cord; muscle weakness, speech/motor/cognitive decline in early childhood[cite: 14]
Phenylketonuria (PKU)[cite: 14] Autosomal recessive mutation in the PAH gene (chromosome 12) preventing breakdown of phenylalanine into tyrosine[cite: 14] Toxic phenylalanine buildup damages brain neurons; causes intellectual disability if untreated, stunted growth, epilepsy, skin rash, mouselike odor[cite: 14]
Hemophilia (A & B)[cite: 14] Mutations in blood-clotting genes located on the X chromosome[cite: 14] Prolonged bleeding after cuts or injuries and dangerous internal bleeding that can damage organs and tissues[cite: 14]
Sickle Cell Anemia (SCA)[cite: 14] Point mutation in the hemoglobin gene (chromosome 11) replacing glutamic acid with valine in β-globin[cite: 14] Red blood cells become sickle- or crescent-shaped and block blood flow, causing pain episodes (arms, legs, chest, abdomen) and frequent infections[cite: 14]
Cystic Fibrosis (CF)[cite: 14] Mutation in a specific gene on chromosome 7 disrupting chloride and sodium transport across epithelium[cite: 14] Thick, sticky mucus clogs lungs, pancreas, liver, and intestines; chronic cough with phlegm, lung infections, weight loss, bowel issues, salty sweat[cite: 14]
Cri-du-chat Syndrome[cite: 15] Deletion of a portion of chromosome 5 ("cat's cry syndrome")[cite: 15] Low birth weight, poor growth, severe cognitive/speech/motor delays, and behavioral challenges[cite: 15]
  • Neutral & Silent Mutations:
    • Neutral Mutations: Occur mostly in noncoding DNA regions and have no noticeable effect on protein production or the organism[cite: 15].
    • Silent Mutations: Change a DNA base without changing the resulting amino acid because the genetic code is redundant (e.g., changing mRNA codon AAG to AAA still codes for lysine)[cite: 15].

3.2 Mutations in Populations

  • Evolution & Genetic Diversity: Over generations, inherited mutations increase genetic variation, generate new adaptations, cause genetic disorders, and drive species evolution[cite: 15, 16].
  • Context Matters (Environment & Organism): A single mutation can be beneficial to one organism yet harmful to another—for example, antibiotic resistance helps bacteria survive antibiotic medication, but makes infections much harder to treat in human patients[cite: 15].
