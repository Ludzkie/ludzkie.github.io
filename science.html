Science Exam Review Notes

1. Earth's Interior

Mariana Trench & Ocean Deep Focus

  • Location & Depth: Situated in the western Pacific Ocean; houses the Challenger Deep (~11,000 m / 36,000 ft deep).
  • Formation Mechanism: Result of oceanic plate subduction where the dense Oceanic Pacific Plate sinks under the smaller Mariana Plate.

Seismic Waves & Detection

  • Seismometer: Instrument that measures and records ground motion caused by seismic waves, generating a seismogram.
  • P-waves (Primary / Compressional): Longitudinal body waves; travel fastest; pass through solids, liquids, and gases.
  • S-waves (Secondary / Shear): Transverse body waves; slower than P-waves; pass only through solids (their blockage by the outer core proved its liquid state).

Compositional vs. Mechanical Layers

Classification Type Layer Name Key Physical & Chemical Characteristics
Compositional Layers
(Based on chemical composition)		 Crust Thin outer silicate layer. Divided into thin, dense Oceanic crust (basaltic) and thick, light Continental crust (granitic).
Mantle Thickest layer (~84% Earth's volume). Composed of ultra-mafic rock rich in iron and magnesium (peridotite).
Core Dense metallic sphere made predominantly of Iron (Fe) and Nickel (Ni).
Mechanical Layers
(Based on physical properties)		 Lithosphere Rigid, brittle outer layer comprising the crust and uppermost mantle; broken into tectonic plates.
Asthenosphere Semi-fluid, ductile layer in the upper mantle; dynamic convection here drives tectonic plate movement.
Mesosphere Lower mantle; solid and rigid due to intense hydrostatic pressure.
Outer Core Liquid metal layer; convective motions generate Earth's magnetic field (geomagnetic dynamo).
Inner Core Solid metallic center held solid by extreme pressure despite temperatures (~6,000 °C).

Sources of Earth's Internal Heat

  • Radiogenic Heat: Continuous thermal release from the radioactive decay of long-lived isotopes (238U, 235U, 232Th, 40K).
  • Primordial Heat: Thermal energy retained from early planetary formation, gravitational accretion, impact bombardment, and core differentiation.

2. Continental Drift & Plate Tectonics

Continental Drift Theory

Proposed by Alfred Wegener (1912), stating that Earth's continents were once connected into a single supercontinent named Pangaea, surrounded by the superocean Panthalassa.

4 Core Evidences of Continental Drift

  1. Continental Shorelines: Complementary fit of coastlines across oceans (e.g., eastern South America fitting western Africa).
  2. Fossils: Shared terrestrial species fossils found across widely separated oceans (e.g., Mesosaurus, Glossopteris, Lystrosaurus).
  3. Matching Rock Formations: Identical rock sequences and mountain chain structures aligned across oceans (e.g., Appalachian Mountains matching Caledonian Mountains).
  4. Paleoclimate Data: Glacial striations and tillites found in tropical continental regions (e.g., India, Australia, South Africa).

Plate Boundaries

Boundary Type Motion Geological Features & Examples
Divergent Plates pull apart ($\leftarrow \rightarrow$) Mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, shallow earthquakes.
Examples: Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift.
Convergent Plates collide ($\rightarrow \leftarrow$) Subduction zones, deep trenches, volcanic arcs, mountain ranges.
Examples: Andes Mountains, Mariana Trench, Himalayas.
Transform Plates slide past each other ($\uparrow \downarrow$) Strike-slip faulting, frequent powerful earthquakes, no crust created or destroyed.
Examples: San Andreas Fault.

Major & Minor Tectonic Plates

  • Major Plates: Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian, South American.
  • Minor Plates: Philippine Sea, Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Arabian, Juan de Fuca, Scotia.

Sea-Floor Spreading & Mid-Ocean Ridges

  • Mechanism (Harry Hess): Upwelling mantle magma erupts at Mid-Ocean Ridges, cools to form new oceanic crust, and pushes older crust outward.
  • Evidence: Symmetric alternating **paleomagnetic reversal stripes** on both sides of mid-ocean ridges, alongside increasing sediment age farther from the ridge axis.

3. Fossils & Stratigraphy

Stratigraphic Principles

  • Law of Superposition: In undeformed sedimentary rock strata, older layers remain at the bottom and younger layers lie near the top.
  • Law of Original Horizontality: Layers of sediment are originally deposited horizontally under action of gravity.
  • Law of Cross-Cutting Relationships: Any geological feature (fault, igneous intrusion) that cuts across another rock layer must be younger than the layer it cuts.

Fossils & Index Fossils

  • Fossilization Process: Preserved remains, traces, or impressions of organisms in sedimentary rocks (e.g., permineralization, molds, casts, trace fossils).
  • Index Fossils: Short-lived, geographically widespread, and abundant organisms used to date and correlate rock layers (e.g., Trilobites, Ammonites).

Dating Methods Comparison

Method Basis & Working Mechanism Output / Purpose
Relative Dating Employs stratigraphy laws, cross-cutting relationships, and index fossils. Establishes sequence of events (determines if a rock is older/younger relative to surrounding layers).
Absolute (Radiometric) Dating Measures radioactive decay ratios of unstable isotopes (e.g., Carbon-14, Potassium-Argon, Uranium-Lead) based on known half-lives. Determines actual numerical age in years.

4. Extended Geologic Time Scale (GTS)

Hierarchy: Eon → Era → Period → Epoch → Age

I. PRECAMBRIAN PRE-EONS / EONS (4.6 Ga – 538.8 Ma)

  • Hadean Eon (4.6 – 4.0 Ga): Formation of Earth, surface volcanism, asteroid impacts, atmosphere formation, ocean condensation, origin of the Moon via impact.
  • Archean Eon (4.0 – 2.5 Ga): First stable continental crusts; atmospheric gases stabilize; emergence of first prokaryotic life and anaerobic stromatolites (cyanobacteria).
  • Proterozoic Eon (2.5 Ga – 538.8 Ma): Great Oxidation Event (GOE); formation of banded iron deposits; development of eukaryotic cells and soft-bodied multicellular Ediacaran biota.

II. PHANEROZOIC EON (538.8 Ma – Present)

1. Paleozoic Era ("Ancient Life": 538.8 – 251.9 Ma)

  • Cambrian Period (538.8 – 485.4 Ma): Cambrian Explosion — rapid evolution of hard-shelled organisms (Trilobites, Brachiopods).
  • Ordovician Period (485.4 – 443.8 Ma): Invertebrate diversification, early marine communities; ends with major glaciation mass extinction.
  • Silurian Period (443.8 – 419.2 Ma): First jawed fishes, expansion of early terrestrial vascular plants (Cooksonia).
  • Devonian Period (419.2 – 358.9 Ma): "Age of Fishes"; emergence of forests and early tetrapods (Tiktaalik).
  • Carboniferous Period (358.9 – 298.9 Ma): Coal swamp forests, giant arthropods, first amniotic reptiles. Divided into Mississippian and Pennsylvanian sub-periods.
  • Permian Period (298.9 – 251.9 Ma): Formation of Pangaea complete; dominated by synapsids; ends with Permian-Triassic Mass Extinction ("The Great Dying").

2. Mesozoic Era ("Age of Reptiles": 251.9 – 66.0 Ma)

  • Triassic Period (251.9 – 201.4 Ma): Emergence of early dinosaurs, gymnosperm domination, first small mammals; Pangaea begins rifting.
  • Jurassic Period (201.4 – 145.0 Ma): Abundant giant sauropods, large theropods, first birds (Archaeopteryx), marine reptiles.
  • Cretaceous Period (145.0 – 66.0 Ma): Flowering plants (Angiosperms) evolve; high dinosaur diversity (T. rex, Triceratops). Ends with K-Pg Mass Extinction (Chicxulub asteroid impact).

3. Cenozoic Era ("Age of Mammals": 66.0 Ma – Present)

A. Paleogene Period (66.0 – 23.03 Ma)
  • Paleocene Epoch (66.0 – 56.0 Ma): Adaptive radiation of placental mammals.
    Ages: Danian, Selandian, Thanetian.
  • Eocene Epoch (56.0 – 33.9 Ma): Global warm climates; evolution of early horses, primitive whales, and bats.
    Ages: Ypresian, Lutetian, Bartonian, Priabonian.
  • Oligocene Epoch (33.9 – 23.03 Ma): World cools; establishment of wide grasslands; early mammalian carnivores.
    Ages: Rupelian, Chattian.
B. Neogene Period (23.03 – 2.58 Ma)
  • Miocene Epoch (23.03 – 5.33 Ma): Grassland ecosystem expansion; development of early hominins.
    Ages: Aquitanian, Burdigalian, Langhian, Serravallian, Tortonian, Messinian.
  • Pliocene Epoch (5.33 – 2.58 Ma): Isthmus of Panama closes; cooling climate; emergence of Australopithecus.
    Ages: Zanclean, Piacenzian.
C. Quaternary Period (2.58 Ma – Present)
  • Pleistocene Epoch (2.58 Ma – 11.7 ka): Glacial-interglacial cycles ("Ice Age"); megafauna thrive (Mammoths, Saber-tooths); evolution of modern Homo sapiens.
    Ages: Gelasian, Calabrian, Chibanian, Upper Pleistocene.
  • Holocene Epoch (11.7 ka – Present): Current post-glacial warm interval; human civilization and agriculture expand.
    Ages: Greenlandian, Northgrippian, Meghalayan.
  • (Proposed Epoch: Anthropocene — marking distinct global human industrial impact).

5. Minor Members of the Solar System

Asteroids

  • Characteristics: Small, rocky, metallic bodies leftover from solar system formation, primarily orbiting in the Asteroid Belt between Mars and Jupiter.
  • Types: Classified by composition: C-type (carbonaceous), S-type (silicaceous/rocky), M-type (metallic).
  • Ceres: Largest asteroid in the main belt (reclassified as a dwarf planet).
  • Near-Earth Asteroids (NEAs): Asteroids with orbits bringing them close to Earth's orbit (e.g., Atira, Aten, Apollo, and Amor groups).

Comets

  • Characteristics: Icy, dusty small celestial bodies with highly eccentric orbits originating from the Kuiper Belt or Oort Cloud.
  • Anatomy of a Comet:
    • Nucleus: Solid, frozen core composed of water ice, frozen gases, and dust.
    • Coma: Temporary halo of gas and dust envelope around the nucleus formed by solar heating.
    • Ion (Gas) Tail: Glowing gas pushed directly away from the Sun by solar wind.
    • Dust Tail: Curved trail of dust grains pushed back by solar radiation pressure.
  • Comet Types:
    • Short-Period Comets: Orbital periods $< 200$ years; originate in the Kuiper Belt (e.g., Halley's Comet).
    • Long-Period Comets: Orbital periods $> 200$ years to millions of years; originate in the Oort Cloud (e.g., Comet Hale-Bopp).
  • Halley's Comet (1P/Halley): Famous short-period comet returning approximately every 75–76 years.

Meteoroids, Meteors, and Meteorites

Term Definition & Location
Meteoroid Small rocky or metallic fragment traveling in outer space.
Meteor Light streak ("shooting star") produced as a meteoroid vaporizes passing through Earth's atmosphere.
Meteorite A remnant of a meteoroid that survives atmospheric passage and strikes Earth's surface.
Meteor Showers Annual events occurring when Earth passes through dusty debris trails left behind by comets (e.g., Perseids, Geminids).

Dwarf Planets

Spherical bodies orbiting the Sun with sufficient mass for hydrostatic equilibrium, but which have not cleared their orbital neighborhood and are not natural satellites.

  • Ceres: Smallest dwarf planet; located inside the main Asteroid Belt.
  • Pluto: Famous Kuiper Belt dwarf planet; orbited by 5 known moons (Charon, Styx, Nix, Kerberos, Hydra).
  • Makemake: Located in the Kuiper Belt; covered in frozen methane, ethane, and nitrogen ice.
  • Haumea: Rapidly rotating Kuiper Belt object with a distinct elongated ellipsoid shape.
  • Eris: Highly massive dwarf planet located in the Scattered Disc region; triggered the IAU 2006 planet definition reclassification.
