Master Math Exam Review Notes: Geometry, Proofs & Triangles

1. Quadrilaterals

Line Relationships & Geometry Basics

Figure 1.1: Parallelism (\(m \parallel n\)) cut by transversal \(t\)
Figure 1.2: Perpendicularity (\(A \perp B\)) forming \(90^\circ\) right angles
  • Parallelism (\(\parallel\)): Two or more lines, rays, or line segments that lie within the same plane and maintain a constant perpendicular distance, ensuring they never intersect regardless of extension length.
  • Perpendicularity (\(\perp\)): Two lines, rays, or line segments that intersect at a single point to form exact \(90^\circ\) right angles.
  • Coplanar Lines: Lines that lie entirely within the exact same two-dimensional flat plane.
  • Skew Lines: Non-coplanar lines that do not intersect and are not parallel (they exist across different three-dimensional planes).
  • Etc. (Transversal Angle Relationships): When a transversal line intersects two parallel lines, specific angle pairs are established:
    • Corresponding Angles: Equal in measure (\(\angle 1 = \angle 5\)).
    • Alternate Interior Angles: Equal in measure (\(\angle 3 = \angle 6\)).
    • Alternate Exterior Angles: Equal in measure (\(\angle 1 = \angle 8\)).
    • Consecutive Interior Angles: Supplementary; sum to \(180^\circ\) (\(\angle 3 + \angle 5 = 180^\circ\)).

What's a Polygon & Quadrilateral

Figure 1.3: General Quadrilateral ABCD (Interior Sum = \(360^\circ\))
  • Polygon: A closed two-dimensional plane figure formed by three or more straight line segments (sides) that intersect only at their endpoints (vertices).
    • Interior Angle Sum Formula: \(S = (n - 2) \times 180^\circ\), where \(n\) represents the total number of sides.
  • Quadrilateral: A four-sided polygon (\(n = 4\)).
    • Interior Angle Sum: \(S = (4 - 2) \times 180^\circ = 360^\circ\). The four interior angles always sum to exactly \(360^\circ\).
Worked Example: Finding a Missing Angle in a Quadrilateral

A quadrilateral has three interior angles measuring \(85^\circ\), \(110^\circ\), and \(95^\circ\). Find the measure of the fourth interior angle \(x\).

Solution: The sum of interior angles in any quadrilateral is \(360^\circ\).

\(85^\circ + 110^\circ + 95^\circ + x = 360^\circ\)

\(290^\circ + x = 360^\circ \implies x = 360^\circ - 290^\circ = 70^\circ\)

Detailed Quadrilateral Breakdown & Visual Characteristics

1. Parallelogram

Figure 1.4: Parallelogram ABCD with bisecting diagonals at M
  • Definition: A quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel opposite sides.
  • Characteristics:
    • Opposite sides are parallel (\(AB \parallel CD\), \(AD \parallel BC\)).
    • Opposite sides are congruent (\(AB \cong CD\), \(AD \cong BC\)).
    • Opposite interior angles are congruent (\(\angle A \cong \angle C\), \(\angle B \cong \angle D\)).
    • Consecutive interior angles are supplementary (\(m\angle A + m\angle B = 180^\circ\)).
    • Diagonals bisect each other at central midpoint \(M\) (\(AM \cong MC\), \(BM \cong MD\)).

2. Rectangle

Figure 1.5: Rectangle ABCD with congruent diagonals (\(AC \cong BD\))
  • Definition: A parallelogram containing four right angles (\(90^\circ\)).
  • Characteristics:
    • Inherits all standard parallelogram properties.
    • Four interior angles equal \(90^\circ\).
    • Diagonals are congruent (\(AC \cong BD\)) and bisect each other.

3. Rhombus

Figure 1.6: Rhombus ABCD with perpendicular diagonals (\(AC \perp BD\))
  • Definition: A parallelogram with four congruent sides.
  • Characteristics:
    • Inherits all standard parallelogram properties.
    • All four sides are equal in length (\(AB = BC = CD = DA\)).
    • Diagonals are perpendicular (\(AC \perp BD\)).
    • Diagonals bisect interior vertex angles.

4. Square

Figure 1.7: Square ABCD combining Rectangle & Rhombus properties
  • Definition: A regular quadrilateral that is both a rectangle (four right angles) and a rhombus (four equal sides).
  • Characteristics:
    • Four congruent sides and four \(90^\circ\) right interior angles.
    • Diagonals are congruent, perpendicular, and bisect vertex angles into \(45^\circ\) segments.

5. Trapezoid & Trapezoid Types

Figure 1.8: Right Trapezoid
Figure 1.9: Isosceles Trapezoid (Equal Legs)
Figure 1.10: Scalene Trapezoid
  • Definition: A quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides (bases). The non-parallel sides are legs.
  • Trapezoid Types:
    • Right Trapezoid: Contains at least two right angles adjacent to the height line.
    • Isosceles Trapezoid: Non-parallel legs are congruent in length, and base angles along each base are equal.
    • Scalene Trapezoid: Neither legs nor interior angles are equal in measure.

6. Kite

Figure 1.11: Kite ABCD with perpendicular diagonals
  • Definition: A quadrilateral with two distinct pairs of adjacent, congruent sides.
  • Characteristics:
    • Two pairs of adjacent equal sides (\(AB \cong AD\), \(BC \cong DC\)).
    • Diagonals intersect at perpendicular \(90^\circ\) angles (\(AC \perp BD\)).
    • Main diagonal bisects the cross diagonal into equal halves.
    • One pair of opposite angles between non-congruent sides is congruent (\(\angle B \cong \angle D\)).

2. Properties of a Parallelogram

Given a standard parallelogram \(ABCD\):

  • Opposite sides: Opposite sides are parallel (\(AB \parallel CD\), \(AD \parallel BC\)) and congruent (\(AB \cong CD\), \(AD \cong BC\)).
  • Opposite angles: Opposite interior angles are congruent (\(\angle A \cong \angle C\), \(\angle B \cong \angle D\)).
  • Consecutive angles: Interior angles following each other along a side are supplementary (\(m\angle A + m\angle B = 180^\circ\)).
  • Adjacent angles: Angles sharing a common side add up to \(180^\circ\).
  • Diagonals: Line segments connecting non-adjacent vertices (\(AC\) and \(BD\)).
  • Bisect: Diagonals bisect each other into equal halves at their mutual midpoint \(M\).
Worked Example: Parallelogram Angle & Side Calculation

In parallelogram \(ABCD\), if \(m\angle A = (4x + 10)^\circ\) and consecutive angle \(m\angle B = (2x + 20)^\circ\), solve for \(x\) and determine the measure of \(\angle C\).

Solution: Consecutive angles in a parallelogram are supplementary.

\((4x + 10) + (2x + 20) = 180^\circ\)

\(6x + 30 = 180 \implies 6x = 150 \implies x = 25\)

Substitute \(x = 25\) into \(m\angle A\): \(m\angle A = 4(25) + 10 = 110^\circ\).

Since opposite angles are congruent, \(m\angle C = m\angle A = 110^\circ\).

3. Trapezoids and Kites

Figure 3.1: Trapezoid Bases, Altitude, and Midsegment/Median

Trapezoid Elements & Formulas

  • Trapezoid Properties: Exactly one pair of parallel bases; consecutive interior angles between distinct bases are supplementary.
  • Bases: The two parallel sides (\(b_1\) and \(b_2\)).
  • Legs: The two non-parallel sides.
  • Altitude: Perpendicular line segment measuring the height distance (\(h\)) between bases.
  • Median (Midsegment): The line segment connecting the midpoints of the two non-parallel legs.
  • Midpoint: The central point that cuts a leg segment into two equal lengths.
Trapezoid Median Formula: \(\text{Median} = \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}\)
Worked Example: Trapezoid Median Calculation

A trapezoid has base lengths measuring \(14\text{ cm}\) and \(22\text{ cm}\). Calculate the length of its median.

Solution:

\(\text{Median} = \frac{14 + 22}{2} = \frac{36}{2} = 18\text{ cm}\).

Isosceles Trapezoids & Kites

  • Isosceles Trapezoids:
    • Non-parallel legs are congruent in length (\(\text{Leg}_1 \cong \text{Leg}_2\)).
    • Base angles along each base are congruent.
    • Diagonals are congruent.
  • Kites:
    • Two pairs of adjacent equal sides.
    • Diagonals intersect perpendicularly (\(d_1 \perp d_2\)).
Kite Area Formula: \(\text{Area} = \frac{d_1 \times d_2}{2}\)

4. Inductive and Deductive Reasoning

Figure 4.1: Inductive Reasoning (Top) vs. Deductive Reasoning (Bottom)
  • Inductive Reasoning: Process of drawing a general conclusion based on repeated specific observations, examples, or patterns.
    • Conjecture: An unproven mathematical statement or hypothesis based on inductive reasoning.
  • Deductive Reasoning: Process of reaching a logically certain conclusion using accepted definitions, postulates, axioms, and proven theorems.
  • Postulates: Fundamental geometry statements accepted as true without formal proof.
  • Theorem: A statement logically proven true using undefined terms, definitions, postulates, and previous theorems.
  • Undefined Terms: Core foundational concepts accepted without formal definitions: Point, Line, and Plane.
  • Axioms: General mathematical rules accepted as true across logic and algebra.

5. Congruent Triangles

Figure 5.1: Triangle Altitude & Median Segments
Figure 5.2: Isosceles Triangle Parts

Triangle Elements & Segments

  • Triangle: A three-sided polygon whose interior angles sum to \(180^\circ\).
  • Angle Bisector: A ray/segment dividing an angle into two congruent angles.
  • Altitude: A perpendicular segment drawn from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side.
  • Median: A segment connecting a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.
  • Midline (Midsegment): A segment connecting midpoints of two sides of a triangle.
    • Midline Theorem: The midline is parallel to the third base side and half its length (\(\text{Midline} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base}\)).
  • Perpendicular Bisector: A line perpendicular to a side at its midpoint.
    • Perpendicular Bisector Theorem: Points on a perpendicular bisector are equidistant from the endpoints.

Isosceles Triangles

  • Legs: The two congruent sides.
  • Base: The non-congruent third side.
  • Vertex Angle: The angle contained between the congruent legs.
  • Base Angles: The two angles adjacent to the base.
    • Base Angles Theorem: If two sides of a triangle are congruent, the angles opposite those sides are congruent.

Congruence Postulates & Right Triangles

Two triangles are congruent (\(\cong\)) if and only if all corresponding pairs of sides are equal in length and all corresponding pairs of angles are equal in measure. The foundational rule for working with congruent figures is CPCTC: Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent.

1. General Triangle Congruence Postulates & Theorems

Rather than measuring all 3 sides and all 3 angles (6 pairs total), geometry allows us to prove two triangles congruent using minimum combinations of 3 corresponding parts.

Figure 5.3: SSS (Side-Side-Side) Postulate
Figure 5.4: SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Postulate
Figure 5.5: ASA (Angle-Side-Angle) Postulate
Figure 5.6: AAS (Angle-Angle-Side) Theorem
  • SSS (Side-Side-Side) Postulate: If three sides of one triangle are congruent to three sides of another triangle, then the two triangles are congruent (\(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF\)).
  • SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Postulate: If two sides and the included angle (the interior angle located directly between those two sides) of one triangle are congruent to two sides and the included angle of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.
  • ASA (Angle-Side-Angle) Postulate: If two angles and the included side (the side shared directly between those two angles) of one triangle are congruent to two angles and the included side of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.
  • AAS (Angle-Angle-Side) Theorem: If two angles and a non-included side of one triangle are congruent to two corresponding angles and non-included side of another triangle, the triangles are congruent.
    *Note on Textbook Classification:* It is categorized as a theorem rather than a postulate because it can be proved logically using the ASA Postulate together with the Triangle Angle Sum Theorem (since knowing two interior angles automatically determines the exact measure of the third angle: \(\angle 3 = 180^\circ - (\angle 1 + \angle 2)\)).
Warning: Invalid Congruence Shortcuts (AAA and SSA)
  • AAA (Angle-Angle-Angle): Proves Similarity (\(\sim\)), NOT Congruence (\(\cong\)). Triangles can have identical angles but completely different side lengths (e.g., an equilateral triangle with side length 2 vs. side length 20).
  • SSA (Side-Side-Angle / "ASS"): Does NOT prove congruence because it creates the "Ambiguous Case." Knowing two side lengths and a non-included angle can form two entirely different geometric triangles (one acute, one obtuse).

2. Right Triangle Congruence Theorems

In a right-angled triangle, the side opposite the \(90^\circ\) right angle is the Hypotenuse (the longest side). The remaining two sides that form the right angle are called Legs. Because the \(90^\circ\) angle is always fixed and known, special simplified congruence theorems apply.

Figure 5.7: LL (Leg-Leg) Theorem
Figure 5.8: LA (Leg-Angle) Theorem
Figure 5.9: HL (Hypotenuse-Leg) Theorem
Figure 5.10: HA (Hypotenuse-Angle) Theorem
  • LL (Leg-Leg) Theorem: If the two legs of one right triangle are congruent to the two legs of another right triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
    *General Postulate Equivalent:* This is a direct application of the SAS Postulate, where the $90^\circ$ right angle acts as the included angle between the two legs.
  • LA (Leg-Angle) Theorem: If a leg and an acute angle of one right triangle are congruent to a leg and an acute angle of another right triangle, the triangles are congruent.
    *General Postulate Equivalent:* Derived from ASA (if the angle is adjacent to the leg) or AAS (if the angle is opposite the leg).
  • HL (Hypotenuse-Leg) Theorem: If the hypotenuse and one leg of a right triangle are congruent to the hypotenuse and one leg of another right triangle, the triangles are congruent.
    *Special Note:* Even though this looks like "SSA" (which is normally invalid), the Pythagorean Theorem (\(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\)) guarantees that if the hypotenuse (\(c\)) and one leg (\(a\)) match, the third leg (\(b = \sqrt{c^2 - a^2}\)) must also match, rendering it equivalent to SSS.
  • HA (Hypotenuse-Angle) Theorem: If the hypotenuse and an acute angle of one right triangle are congruent to the hypotenuse and an acute angle of another right triangle, the triangles are congruent.
    *General Postulate Equivalent:* Derived directly from the AAS Theorem.
Worked Example: Identifying Right Triangle Congruence

Two right triangles \(\triangle PQR\) and \(\triangle STU\) both have a right angle at vertices \(Q\) and \(T\). You are given that hypotenuse \(PR = 13\text{ cm}\), hypotenuse \(SU = 13\text{ cm}\), acute angle \(m\angle P = 35^\circ\), and acute angle \(m\angle S = 35^\circ\).

Question: Are the triangles congruent, and which theorem proves it?

Solution: Yes, \(\triangle PQR \cong \triangle STU\). Since both triangles share a congruent hypotenuse (\(PR \cong SU = 13\text{ cm}\)) and a congruent acute angle (\(\angle P \cong \angle S = 35^\circ\)), they are congruent by the HA (Hypotenuse-Angle) Theorem (or AAS).

6. Constructing Segments, Angles, Triangles, and Two-Column Proofs

  • Geometric Construction: Drawing accurate figures using only an unmarked straightedge and a compass (copying segments/angles, perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, altitudes, medians).
  • Two-Column Proofs: A structured table organizing logical arguments step-by-step:
    • Statement Column (Left): Sequential claims leading from given information to the final conclusion.
    • Reason Column (Right): Formal mathematical justifications (Givens, Definitions, Postulates, Theorems).
Sample Two-Column Proof: Proving Congruent Triangles

Given: \(AB \parallel CD\), \(AB \cong CD\)

Prove: \(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA\)

Statements (Left Column) Reasons (Right Column)
1. \(AB \parallel CD\), \(AB \cong CD\) 1. Given
2. \(\angle BAC \cong \angle DCA\) 2. Alternate Interior Angles Theorem
3. \(AC \cong AC\) 3. Reflexive Property of Congruence
4. \(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA\) 4. SAS Postulate (Side-Angle-Side)
