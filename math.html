Math Exam Review Notes: Quadrilaterals, Proofs & Triangles

1. Quadrilaterals

Fundamental Geometric Relationships

  • Parallelism (\(\parallel\)): Two or more coplanar lines, rays, or segments that maintain a constant distance apart and never intersect, regardless of extension distance.
    • Parallel Line Postulate: If two parallel lines are cut by a transversal, alternate interior angles are equal (\(\angle 1 \cong \angle 2\)), and consecutive interior angles are supplementary (\(m\angle 1 + m\angle 3 = 180^\circ\)).
  • Perpendicularity (\(\perp\)): Lines, segments, or rays that intersect at a single point to form four right angles (\(90^\circ\)).
    • Perpendicular Line Theorem: Two intersecting lines form four \(90^\circ\) angles. If two lines are perpendicular to the same line, they are parallel to each other.
  • Coplanar Lines: Lines that lie completely within the same flat two-dimensional surface (plane).
  • Skew Lines: Non-coplanar lines that do not intersect and are not parallel (they exist only in 3D space across different planes).

Polygons & Quadrilaterals

  • Polygon: A closed plane figure bounded by at least three straight line segments (sides) intersecting at non-collinear endpoints (vertices).
    • Polygon Interior Angle Sum Formula: \(S = (n - 2) \times 180^\circ\), where \(n\) is the number of sides.
  • Quadrilateral: A 4-sided polygon (\(n = 4\)).
    • Interior Angle Sum: \(S = (4 - 2) \times 180^\circ = 360^\circ\). The four interior angles always sum to \(360^\circ\).

Classification of Quadrilaterals

Shape Type Defining Criteria Key Mathematical Properties
Parallelogram 2 pairs of parallel opposite sides Opposite sides \(\cong\), opposite angles \(\cong\), consecutive angles supplementary, diagonals bisect each other.
Rectangle Parallelogram with 4 right angles (\(90^\circ\)) Inherits all parallelogram properties + 4 equal angles + diagonals are congruent (\(AC \cong BD\)).
Rhombus Parallelogram with 4 equal sides Inherits all parallelogram properties + 4 equal sides + diagonals are perpendicular (\(AC \perp BD\)) and bisect vertex angles.
Square Regular quadrilateral (both Rectangle & Rhombus) 4 equal sides + 4 right angles. Diagonals are congruent, perpendicular, and bisect vertex angles into \(45^\circ\) segments.
Trapezoid Exactly 1 pair of parallel sides Parallel sides are bases, non-parallel sides are legs. Consecutive interior angles between distinct bases are supplementary.
Isosceles Trapezoid Trapezoid with congruent non-parallel legs Legs \(\cong\), base angles along each base \(\cong\), diagonals \(\cong\), opposite angles supplementary.
Kite 2 distinct pairs of adjacent congruent sides Diagonals \(\perp\), main diagonal bisects cross diagonal, one pair of opposite angles \(\cong\), main diagonal bisects vertex angles.

2. Properties of a Parallelogram

Given a parallelogram \(ABCD\):

  • Opposite Sides Theorem:
    • \(AB \parallel CD\) and \(AD \parallel BC\) (By Definition).
    • \(AB \cong CD\) and \(AD \cong BC\) (Opposite sides are congruent).
  • Opposite Angles Theorem:
    • \(\angle A \cong \angle C\) and \(\angle B \cong \angle D\) (Opposite interior angles are congruent).
  • Consecutive / Adjacent Angles Theorem:
    • Consecutive interior angles sharing a common side are supplementary:
      \(m\angle A + m\angle B = 180^\circ\), \(m\angle B + m\angle C = 180^\circ\), \(m\angle C + m\angle D = 180^\circ\), \(m\angle D + m\angle A = 180^\circ\).
  • Diagonals Bisect Theorem:
    • The diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) intersect at a central midpoint \(M\).
    • \(AM \cong MC\) and \(BM \cong MD\) (Diagonals bisect each other into two equal halves).
    • Each diagonal divides the parallelogram into two congruent triangles (\(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA\)).

3. Trapezoids and Kites

Trapezoid Elements & Theorems

  • Bases (\(b_1, b_2\)): The two parallel sides of a trapezoid (\(AB \parallel CD\)).
  • Legs: The two non-parallel sides (\(AD\) and \(BC\)).
  • Altitude (\(h\)): The perpendicular line distance drawn from any point on one base line to the opposite base line.
  • Midpoint: The exact point dividing a segment into two equal lengths.
  • Median (Midsegment): The line segment connecting the midpoints of the non-parallel legs.
Trapezoid Midsegment Length Theorem: \(\text{Median} = \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}\)
Property: The median/midsegment of a trapezoid is always parallel to both bases (\(\text{Median} \parallel b_1 \parallel b_2\)).

Isosceles Trapezoid Special Properties

An isosceles trapezoid is a trapezoid whose non-parallel legs are equal in length (\(AD \cong BC\)).

  • Legs: Non-parallel side lengths are congruent (\(AD = BC\)).
  • Base Angles Theorem: Base angles along the same base line are congruent (\(\angle A \cong \angle B\) and \(\angle C \cong \angle D\)).
  • Diagonals Theorem: Diagonals connecting opposite vertices are congruent (\(AC \cong BD\)).
  • Opposite Angles: Opposite angles are supplementary (\(m\angle A + m\angle C = 180^\circ\) and \(m\angle B + m\angle D = 180^\circ\)).

Kite Properties & Theorems

  • Adjacent Sides: Two separate pairs of adjacent, equal sides (\(AB \cong AD\) and \(BC \cong DC\)). Opposite sides are not congruent.
  • Perpendicular Diagonals: Diagonals intersect at exact \(90^\circ\) angles (\(AC \perp BD\)).
  • Diagonal Bisector: The primary symmetry diagonal (\(AC\)) bisects the secondary diagonal (\(BD\)).
  • Opposite Angles: Exactly one pair of opposite angles (the angles between the non-congruent sides) is congruent (\(\angle B \cong \angle D\)).
  • Angle Bisectors: The main diagonal bisects its vertex interior angles (\(\angle A\) and \(\angle C\)).
Kite Area Formula: \(\text{Area} = \frac{d_1 \times d_2}{2}\) (where \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) are the diagonal lengths)

4. Inductive and Deductive Reasoning

Logic & Reasoning Methods

Feature Inductive Reasoning Deductive Reasoning
Process Moving from specific observations/examples to a general rule. Moving from general established rules/principles to a specific logical outcome.
Output Generates a Conjecture (an unproven hypothesis based on trends). Produces a Logical Proof / Certainty (proven true if premises are true).
Example "The last 4 quadrilaterals drawn had sum \(360^\circ\), so all quadrilaterals must sum to \(360^\circ\)." "All quadrilaterals have angle sums of \(360^\circ\). Figure A is a quadrilateral, so its angles sum to \(360^\circ\)."

Foundational Terms in Geometry

  • Undefined Terms: Core foundational concepts accepted without formal definitions because they are self-evident building blocks:
    • Point: Indicates a location in space; has no dimension (no length, width, or depth).
    • Line: A 1-dimensional continuous path of infinite points extending endlessly in two opposite directions.
    • Plane: A 2-dimensional flat surface extending endlessly in all directions.
  • Axioms: General mathematical rules accepted as true without proof across all fields of mathematics (e.g., Transitive Property: If \(a = b\) and \(b = c\), then \(a = c\)).
  • Postulates: Fundamental geometry statements accepted as true without proof (e.g., Segment Addition Postulate: If \(B\) is between \(A\) and \(C\), then \(AB + BC = AC\)).
  • Theorem: A statement or principle that has been formally proven using logical deductive arguments, undefined terms, axioms, postulates, and existing theorems.

5. Congruent Triangles

Triangle Components & Key Line Segments

  • Triangle (\(\triangle\)): A 3-sided polygon with interior angles summing to \(180^\circ\).
  • Angle Bisector: A segment or ray that divides an interior angle into two equal congruent angles.
  • Altitude: A perpendicular segment drawn from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side (defines the height \(h\)).
  • Median: A segment extending from a vertex to the exact midpoint of the opposite side.
  • Midline (Midsegment): A segment connecting the midpoints of two sides of a triangle.
    • Midline Theorem: The midline connecting midpoints of two sides is parallel to the third base side and measures exactly half its length (\(\text{Midline} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base}\)).
  • Perpendicular Bisector: A line or segment perpendicular to a side that cuts that side into two equal halves at its midpoint.
    • Perpendicular Bisector Theorem: Any point located on the perpendicular bisector of a segment is equidistant from the segment's two endpoints.

Isosceles Triangles

  • Legs: The two congruent sides.
  • Base: The non-congruent third side.
  • Vertex Angle: The angle contained between the two congruent legs.
  • Base Angles: The two angles adjacent to the base.
  • Isosceles Triangle Theorems:
    • Base Angles Theorem: If two sides of a triangle are congruent, then the angles opposite those sides are congruent (\(\angle B \cong \angle C\)).
    • Converse of Base Angles Theorem: If two angles of a triangle are congruent, then the sides opposite those angles are congruent.

General Triangle Congruence Postulates & Theorems

Two triangles are congruent (\(\cong\)) when all corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal (CPCTC: Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent).

Postulate / Theorem Criteria Explanation
SSS Postulate Side-Side-Side Three sides of one triangle are congruent to three sides of another triangle.
SAS Postulate Side-Angle-Side Two sides and the included angle (the angle between the sides) are congruent.
ASA Postulate Angle-Side-Angle Two angles and the included side (the side between the angles) are congruent.
AAS Theorem Angle-Angle-Side Two angles and a non-included side are congruent.
(Note: Labeled a theorem because it is mathematically proven by combining the ASA Postulate with the Triangle Angle Sum Theorem).

Right Triangle Congruence Theorems

In a right triangle, the side opposite the \(90^\circ\) angle is the Hypotenuse, and the remaining sides are Legs.

  • LL Theorem (Leg-Leg): If two legs of one right triangle are congruent to two legs of another right triangle, the triangles are congruent (special case of SAS).
  • LA Theorem (Leg-Angle): If one leg and an acute angle of a right triangle are congruent to a leg and an acute angle of another, the triangles are congruent (special case of ASA or AAS).
  • HL Theorem (Hypotenuse-Leg): If the hypotenuse and one leg of a right triangle are congruent to the hypotenuse and one leg of another, the triangles are congruent.
  • HA Theorem (Hypotenuse-Angle): If the hypotenuse and an acute angle of one right triangle are congruent to the hypotenuse and an acute angle of another, the triangles are congruent (special case of AAS).

6. Geometric Constructions & Two-Column Proofs

Geometric Construction

The precise drawing of geometric shapes, bisectors, and lines using strictly two classical tools:

  1. Straightedge (Unmarked Ruler): Used exclusively to draw straight lines through points.
  2. Compass: Used exclusively to draw arcs and circles of defined radii.
  • Key Constructions: Copying a segment, copying an angle, bisecting an angle, constructing a perpendicular bisector, constructing an altitude, and drawing parallel lines.

Two-Column Proofs

A formal structured table used to organize deductive arguments step-by-step to prove geometric theorems or statements.
