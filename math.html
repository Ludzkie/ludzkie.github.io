Quadratic Functions and Their Key Features

Definition and Forms

  • Quadratic Function: A mathematical relationship in which the highest exponent (degree) of the independent variable is 2[cite: 1, 6].
  • Standard / General Form: Commonly expressed as $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ (or $y = ax^2 + bx + c$), where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants (real numbers) and $a \neq 0$[cite: 1, 3, 14].
  • Graph (Parabola): Unlike linear functions that produce a straight line, a quadratic function produces a symmetrical U-shaped curve called a parabola[cite: 1, 6].
  • Direction of Opening ($a$):
    • If $a > 0$, the parabola opens upward[cite: 1, 3].
    • If $a < 0$, the parabola opens downward[cite: 1, 3].

Vertex is the minimum point[cite: 1, 6].
Opens Downward ($a < 0$)
Vertex is the maximum point[cite: 1, 6].

Key Features of a Parabola

  • Vertex: The turning point where the parabola changes direction (from decreasing to increasing when opening upward, or from increasing to decreasing when opening downward)[cite: 2, 6]. Its $y$- or $k$-coordinate in $(x, y)$ or $(h, k)$ represents the maximum or minimum value of the function[cite: 2, 6].
  • Axis (Line) of Symmetry: The vertical line passing through the vertex that divides the parabola into two equal mirror-image halves, with equation $x = h$[cite: 2, 6].
  • $x$-Intercept(s) / Zeros / Roots: The point(s) where the graph intersects the horizontal $x$-axis (where $y = 0$ or $f(x) = 0$)[cite: 2, 3, 6, 26]. A parabola can have:
    • No $x$-intercepts if it does not meet the $x$-axis[cite: 2];
    • One $x$-intercept if it touches the $x$-axis at one point (tangent to the $x$-axis)[cite: 2]; or
    • Two $x$-intercepts if it intersects the $x$-axis at two distinct points[cite: 2].
  • $y$-Intercept: The point where the graph crosses the vertical $y$-axis, representing the value of $y$ when $x = 0$ (equal to $c$ in standard form)[cite: 2, 3, 6].

Step-by-Step Example: Finding Features from Standard Form

Given: $y = x^2 - 6x + 9$, where $a = 1$, $b = -6$, and $c = 9$[cite: 3].

  1. Opening & Minimum: Since $a = 1 > 0$, the parabola opens upward, so the vertex is the minimum point[cite: 3].
  2. Find $x$-coordinate of the vertex: $$x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-6}{2(1)} = \frac{6}{2} = 3 \quad \text{[cite: 3]}$$
  3. Substitute $x = 3$ to find $y$: $$y = (3)^2 - 6(3) + 9 = 9 - 18 + 9 = 0 \quad \text{[cite: 3]}$$ Vertex: $(3, 0)$; Minimum value: $0$[cite: 3].
  4. Find the $y$-intercept ($x = 0$): Since $c = 9$, the graph passes through $(0, 9)$[cite: 3, 4].
  5. Find the $x$-intercept ($y = 0$): $$x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0 \implies \sqrt{(x - 3)^2} = \sqrt{0} \implies x - 3 = 0 \implies x = 3 \quad \text{[cite: 4]}$$ The graph touches the $x$-axis at $(3, 0)$[cite: 4].

Transforming Between General Form and Vertex Form

A. General Form $\rightarrow$ Perfect Square Trinomial (PST) $\rightarrow$ Vertex Form

Transforming $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ to $y = a(x - h)^2 + k$ clearly identifies the maximum or minimum value and helps in graphing and modeling real-world systems[cite: 7, 8, 14].

  1. Regroup: Group the terms containing $x$ in parentheses[cite: 8, 14].
  2. Complete the Square (PST): Compute $\text{last term} = \left(\frac{\text{middle term}}{2}\right)^2$ to create a Perfect Square Trinomial (PST)[cite: 8, 9, 14].
  3. Subtract & Simplify: Subtract the number added to the grouped terms from the constant outside the parentheses, then simplify[cite: 8, 9, 14].
  4. Factor as Square of a Binomial: Rewrite the PST inside the parentheses as the square of a binomial[cite: 8, 9, 14].

Example A1: Transform $y = x^2 + 8x + 5$ into vertex form[cite: 8].

  1. $y = (x^2 + 8x) + 5$[cite: 8]
  2. $\text{last term} = \left(\frac{8}{2}\right)^2 = (4)^2 = 16 \implies \text{PST: } x^2 + 8x + 16$[cite: 8]
  3. $y = (x^2 + 8x + 16) + 5 - 16 = (x^2 + 8x + 16) - 11$[cite: 9]
  4. Vertex Form: $y = (x + 4)^2 - 11$[cite: 9]

Example A2: Transform $y = x^2 - 2x - 6$ into vertex form[cite: 9].

  1. $y = (x^2 - 2x) - 6$[cite: 9]
  2. $\text{last term} = \left(\frac{-2}{2}\right)^2 = (-1)^2 = 1 \implies \text{PST: } x^2 - 2x + 1$[cite: 9]
  3. $y = (x^2 - 2x + 1) - (6 - 1) = (x^2 - 2x + 1) - 7$[cite: 9]
  4. Vertex Form: $y = (x - 1)^2 - 7$[cite: 9]

B. Vertex Form $\rightarrow$ General Form

  1. Expand Binomial: Rewrite the square of a binomial in expanded form[cite: 10, 14].
  2. Multiply & Combine Like Terms: Multiply by the coefficient $a$ of the grouped terms and combine like terms to simplify[cite: 10, 11, 14].

Example B1: Transform $y = (x - 7)^2 + 3$ into general form[cite: 10].

  1. $y = (x - 7)(x - 7) + 3 = x^2 - 7x - 7x + 49 + 3 = x^2 - 14x + 49 + 3$[cite: 10]
  2. General Form: $y = x^2 - 14x + 52$[cite: 10]

Example B2: Transform $y = 2(x + 5)^2 - 12$ into general form[cite: 10].

  1. $y = 2(x + 5)(x + 5) - 12 = 2(x^2 + 5x + 5x + 25) - 12 = 2(x^2 + 10x + 25) - 12$[cite: 10]
  2. General Form: $y = 2x^2 + 20x + 50 - 12 \implies \mathbf{y = 2x^2 + 20x + 38}$[cite: 11]

Sketching Graphs, Parameter Effects, and Zeros

Methods of Sketching a Quadratic Graph

  • Using a Table of Values: Choose $x$-values (ideally including negative numbers, zero, and positive numbers), compute corresponding $y$-values using the function, plot the points on the Cartesian plane, and connect them smoothly to form a parabola[cite: 11, 12, 23].
  • Using Equations / Key Features: Determine the vertex $(h, k)$ (by transforming to vertex form or using $h = -\frac{b}{2a}$ and $k = -\frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a}$), solve for $x$-intercepts ($y = 0$) and $y$-intercept ($x = 0$), plot the points, and connect them to form a parabola[cite: 12, 13, 25].

Effects of Parameters $a$, $h$, and $k$ on $y = a(x - h)^2 + k$

  • Effect of $a$: Affects vertical stretch/compression and opening direction[cite: 19, 26]. Larger $|a|$ (e.g., $a = 3$) makes the graph narrower; smaller $0 < |a| < 1$ (e.g., $a = \frac{1}{3}$) makes it wider[cite: 19, 26]. If $a > 0$, it opens upward; if $a < 0$, it opens downward[cite: 19, 26].
  • Effect of $h$: Controls horizontal position of the vertex and axis of symmetry ($x = h$) along the $x$-axis without changing shape or orientation[cite: 18, 22, 26]. If $h > 0$, it shifts right; if $h < 0$, it shifts left[cite: 18, 26].
  • Effect of $k$: Controls vertical position of the vertex along the $y$-axis without affecting width or opening direction[cite: 17, 26]. If $k > 0$, it shifts upward; if $k < 0$, it shifts downward[cite: 17, 26].
Effect of $a$
Larger $|a|$ is narrower; smaller $|a|$ is wider; negative $a$ flips downward[cite: 19, 26].
Graph of $y = x^2 - 8x + 7$
Vertex $(4, -9)$, $x$-intercepts $(1, 0), (7, 0)$, $y$-intercept $(0, 7)$[cite: 13, 15].

Step-by-Step Example: Sketching from General Form

Given: Sketch the graph of $y = x^2 - 8x + 7$ ($a = 1$, $b = -8$, $c = 7$)[cite: 13].

  1. Find the vertex $(h, k)$: $$h = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-8}{2(1)} = 4 \quad \text{[cite: 13]}$$ $$k = -\frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a} = -\frac{(-8)^2 - 4(1)(7)}{4(1)} = -\frac{64 - 28}{4} = -\frac{36}{4} = -9 \quad \text{[cite: 13]}$$ Vertex: $(4, -9)$; Axis of symmetry: $x = 4$[cite: 13, 15].
  2. Find the $x$-intercepts ($y = 0$): $$0 = x^2 - 8x + 7 \implies 0 = (x - 1)(x - 7) \implies x = 1 \text{ or } x = 7 \quad \text{[cite: 13]}$$ $x$-intercepts: $(1, 0)$ and $(7, 0)$[cite: 13, 15].
  3. Find the $y$-intercept ($x = 0$): $$y = (0)^2 - 8(0) + 7 = 7 \implies \mathbf{(0, 7)} \quad \text{[cite: 15]}$$
  4. Plot & Connect: Plot the vertex, intercepts, and symmetric points on the Cartesian plane and connect them smoothly to form an upward-opening parabola[cite: 13, 15].

Finding Zeros (Roots / $x$-Intercepts) in Factored Form

For $f(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)$, the zeros are the values of $x$ where $f(x) = 0$[cite: 26, 28].

Example: Find the zeros of $f(x) = 5(x - 3)(x + 2)$[cite: 28].

  1. Set $f(x) = 0$ and divide both sides by $5$: $0 = 5(x - 3)(x + 2) \implies 0 = (x - 3)(x + 2)$[cite: 28].
  2. Set each factor equal to zero: $x - 3 = 0 \implies x = 3$, and $x + 2 = 0 \implies x = -2$[cite: 28].
  3. Zeros / $x$-intercepts: $x = 3$ and $x = -2$, corresponding to $(3, 0)$ and $(-2, 0)$[cite: 28].

Solving Quadratic Equations and Word Problems

A quadratic equation in general form is $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ ($a \neq 0$), and the values of the variable that make it true are called roots or solutions[cite: 31].

A. Extracting Square Roots (Square Root Property)

  • Useful when the squared term is isolated (no linear $x$ term)[cite: 31].
  • For $x^2 = k$: if $k > 0$, two real solutions $x = \sqrt{k}$ or $x = -\sqrt{k}$; if $k = 0$, one real solution $x = 0$; if $k < 0$, no real number solution[cite: 31].

Example: Solve $(y + 2)^2 = 81$[cite: 32].

  1. Take square roots on both sides: $\sqrt{(y + 2)^2} = \sqrt{81} \implies y + 2 = \pm 9$[cite: 32].
  2. Solve both cases: $y + 2 = 9 \implies y = 7$, and $y + 2 = -9 \implies y = -11$[cite: 32].
  3. Roots: $y = 7$ or $y = -11$[cite: 32].

B. Factoring (Zero Factor Property)

  • Zero Factor Property: If $ab = 0$, then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$[cite: 32].

Example: Solve $x(3x + 6) = 9$[cite: 34].

  1. Expand and write in standard form: $3x^2 + 6x = 9 \implies 3x^2 + 6x - 9 = 0$[cite: 34].
  2. Divide all terms by GCF $3$: $x^2 + 2x - 3 = 0$[cite: 34].
  3. Factor (factors of $-3$ whose sum is $2$ are $-1$ and $3$): $(x - 1)(x + 3) = 0$[cite: 34].
  4. Equate each factor to zero: $x - 1 = 0 \implies x = 1$; $x + 3 = 0 \implies x = -3$[cite: 34].
  5. Solution Set: $\{1, -3\}$[cite: 34].

C. Quadratic Formula

For $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ ($a \neq 0$)[cite: 31]:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \quad \text{[cite: 40]}$$

Example: Solve $8d^2 - 22d = -15$[cite: 39].

  1. Standard form: $8d^2 - 22d + 15 = 0$, so $a = 8$, $b = -22$, $c = 15$[cite: 39].
  2. Substitute into the quadratic formula: $$d = \frac{-(-22) \pm \sqrt{(-22)^2 - 4(8)(15)}}{2(8)} = \frac{22 \pm \sqrt{484 - 480}}{16} = \frac{22 \pm \sqrt{4}}{16} = \frac{22 \pm 2}{16} \quad \text{[cite: 39]}$$
  3. Solve both cases: $d = \frac{22 + 2}{16} = \frac{24}{16} = \frac{3}{2}$, and $d = \frac{22 - 2}{16} = \frac{20}{16} = \frac{5}{4}$[cite: 39].
  4. Solution Set: $\left\{\frac{3}{2}, \frac{5}{4}\right\}$[cite: 39].

D. Word Problem Example

Problem: A garden is made of a square section of side $x\text{ m}$ and an adjacent rectangle of dimensions $x$ by $(x + 4)\text{ m}$. The total area is $160\text{ m}^2$. Find $x$[cite: 37].

  1. Total area: $x^2 + x(x + 4) = x^2 + x^2 + 4x = 2x^2 + 4x$[cite: 37].
  2. Set equal to $160$: $2x^2 + 4x = 160 \implies 2x^2 + 4x - 160 = 0$[cite: 37].
  3. Divide by $2$: $x^2 + 2x - 80 = 0 \implies (x + 10)(x - 8) = 0$[cite: 37].
  4. Solve: $x = -10$ (reject, since length cannot be negative) or $x = 8$[cite: 37].
  5. Answer: $x = 8\text{ meters}$[cite: 37].

Similarity of Polygons, Triangles, and Special Right Triangles

Similar vs. Congruent Figures

  • Similar Polygons: Polygons with the same shape but not necessarily the same size; corresponding interior angles are congruent (equal) and corresponding side lengths are proportional with a constant ratio called the scale factor[cite: 41]. Written using $\sim$ (e.g., $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle PQR$)[cite: 41].
  • Congruent Figures: Figures that are exact in both shape and size, with all corresponding sides and angles equal[cite: 41].

Triangle Similarity Theorems & Right Triangle Similarity

  • AA Similarity Theorem (Angle-Angle): Two triangles are similar if two pairs of corresponding angles are congruent[cite: 43].
  • SSS Similarity Theorem (Side-Side-Side): Two triangles are similar if their corresponding side lengths are proportional[cite: 43].
  • SAS Similarity Theorem (Side-Angle-Side): Two triangles are similar if two pairs of corresponding sides are proportional and the included angles are congruent[cite: 44].
  • Right Triangle Similarity: If an altitude is drawn from the right angle to the hypotenuse of a right triangle, the two smaller triangles formed are similar to each other and to the original triangle ($\triangle ACB \sim \triangle ADC \sim \triangle CDB$)[cite: 44, 45].

Direct and Inverse Variation

A. Direct Variation

  • Definition: A relationship where $y$ increases as $x$ increases (or both decrease together), written as $y = kx$ ($k \neq 0$), where $k$ is the constant of variation ($k = \frac{y}{x}$)[cite: 46, 47, 58].
  • Graph & Proportion: Its graph is a straight line passing through the origin $(0, 0)$, and a proportion is $\frac{y_2}{y_1} = \frac{x_2}{x_1}$[cite: 46, 51, 58].
Direct Variation Graph ($y = kx$)
A straight line passing through the origin $(0, 0)$[cite: 46, 58].

Example (Direct Variation): If $y$ varies directly as $x$, and $y = 4$ when $x = 2$, find $y$ when $x = 7$[cite: 52].

  1. Find $k$: $4 = k(2) \implies k = 2$[cite: 52].
  2. Substitute $k = 2$ and $x = 7$ into $y = 2x$: $y = 2(7) = \mathbf{14}$[cite: 53].

B. Inverse Variation

  • Definition: A relationship where two variables vary in opposite directions (as one increases, the other decreases), written as $y = \frac{k}{x}$ ($k$ is a constant, $k = xy$)[cite: 47, 54, 58].
  • Inverse Proportion: Since $x_1 y_1 = x_2 y_2$, the proportion is $\frac{y_2}{y_1} = \frac{x_1}{x_2}$[cite: 54, 56].

Example (Inverse Variation): If $10$ painters can paint a mall wall in $8$ days, how many days will it take $16$ painters[cite: 56]? Let $c =$ number of painters and $d =$ number of days[cite: 56].

  1. Use $c = \frac{k}{d}$: $10 = \frac{k}{8} \implies k = 80$[cite: 56].
  2. For $c = 16$: $16 = \frac{80}{d} \implies d = \frac{80}{16} = \mathbf{5\text{ days}}$[cite: 56, 57].
Study Tip: Always check the sign of coefficients, balance terms carefully when completing the square, and verify whether solutions make sense in word problems (such as rejecting negative lengths)[cite: 8, 37].
