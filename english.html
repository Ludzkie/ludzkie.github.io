English Exam Review Notes

1. Argumentative Text & Linguistic Features

An argumentative text aims to persuade the reader to adopt a specific point of view or take action by presenting clear claims supported by evidence and reasoning.

Key Linguistic Features

  • Modal Verbs: Express necessity, possibility, or obligation (e.g., must, should, could, might).
  • Evaluative Language: Words that convey judgment or value (e.g., essential, crucial, problematic, beneficial).
  • Declarative Sentences: Stating facts or claims directly to assert authority.
  • Passive Voice (Selective Use): Used to create an objective, formal tone when emphasizing the action rather than the actor (e.g., "Mistakes were made" vs. "We made mistakes").
  • Rhetorical Questions: Questions asked for effect to engage or prompt reflection rather than expect an answer.

2. Types of Target Audiences

Understanding your audience determines the tone, complexity, vocabulary, and evidence used in writing.

Audience Type Characteristics Key Strategy / Tone
Expert / Professional Possesses specialized, high-level knowledge of the topic. Formal tone, specialized technical jargon, precise empirical data.
Laypeople / General Has little to no prior background knowledge. Clear language, relatable analogies, plain definitions, high readability.

3. Transition Devices

Transition devices create logical flow and cohesion between sentences and paragraphs.

  • Addition: furthermore, moreover, in addition, besides
  • Contrast / Concession: however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in contrast, despite
  • Cause & Effect: consequently, therefore, as a result, thus, hence
  • Illustration / Example: for instance, specifically, to illustrate, namely
  • Sequence / Time: initially, subsequently, meanwhile, finally

4. Techniques in Organizing Information

Organizing ideas logically helps readers follow complex reasoning.

  • Chronological Order: Sequence of events according to time (ideal for narratives, historical analysis, process steps).
  • Spatial Order: Arranging details by physical location or layout (top-to-bottom, left-to-right).
  • Order of Importance (Hierarchical): Presenting points from most critical to least critical (or climax building from least to most).
  • Compare & Contrast: Grouping similarities and differences side-by-side or point-by-point.
  • Problem-Solution: Stating an issue clearly followed by proposed actionable resolutions.

5. Graphic Organizers

Visual frameworks used to organize information, analyze concepts, and map relationships between ideas:

  • Venn Diagram: Used for comparing and contrasting. Consists of overlapping circles to show similarities in the intersection and unique differences in the outer sections.
  • Flowchart / Sequence Chart: Maps out a step-by-step process or sequential order of events in a linear progression.
  • Multi-Flow Map: Used for analyzing cause and effect relationships. The central event is placed in the middle box, causes are listed in boxes on the left, and effects/consequences are listed in boxes on the right.
  • Spider Map (Web Map): Used for describing or detailing a central concept. The central idea is placed in the middle, and supporting details or characteristics branch out like legs.
  • Fishbone Diagram (Ishikawa Diagram): Identifies root causes leading to a specific problem or effect, organizing causes into distinct branches/categories.
  • Concept Map: Displays relationships between concepts, connecting main ideas to subtopics using lines or hierarchical branching nodes.

6. Sentence Functions

Sentences serve distinct communicative purposes based on their punctuation and intent:

  1. Declarative: Makes a statement or states a fact. Ends with a period (e.g., "Regular sleep improves cognitive retention.").
  2. Interrogative: Asks a question. Ends with a question mark (e.g., "How do transition devices enhance flow?").
  3. Imperative: Gives a command or makes a request. Ends with a period or exclamation mark (e.g., "Organize your main ideas before writing.").
  4. Exclamatory: Expresses strong emotion or emphasis. Ends with an exclamation mark (e.g., "What a compelling argument that was!").

7. Sentence Structure

Sentence structures vary based on the combination of independent clauses (IC) and dependent clauses (DC).

Simple (1 IC) | Compound (2+ IC) | Complex (1 IC + 1+ DC) | Compound-Complex (2+ IC + 1+ DC)
  • Simple Sentence: One independent clause.
    • Example: "The student reviewed her lecture notes daily."
  • Compound Sentence: Two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So) or a semicolon.
    • Example: "The exam was difficult, but the students were well-prepared."
  • Complex Sentence: One independent clause and at least one dependent clause joined by a subordinating conjunction (because, although, if, when).
    • Example: "Although the topic was complex, she scored well on the test."
  • Compound-Complex Sentence: At least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.
    • Example: "Because the exam was postponed, the class studied extra hours, and everyone felt more confident."

8. Fact, Opinion, and Claims

  • Fact: A statement that can be proven true or false using objective evidence, measurements, or verifiable records (e.g., "Water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure.").
  • Opinion: A subjective expression of personal beliefs, feelings, or values that cannot be objectively verified (e.g., "Literature is the most enjoyable subject in school.").
  • Claim: An arguable assertion or main proposition made in a text that requires logical reasoning and supporting evidence to establish validity.

9. Types of Claims

Claims form the core backbone of any persuasive or argumentative writing:

Type of Claim Definition & Purpose Example
Claim of Fact Asserts that something is true or false, exists, or happened based on verifiable evidence. "Global temperature increases over the last century are driven by human activity."
Claim of Value Asserts a judgment about whether something is good or bad, moral or immoral, desirable or undesirable based on criteria. "Prioritizing mental health education in high schools is more important than standardized testing."
Claim of Policy Advocates for a specific course of action or policy change, usually using words like should or must. "Schools should prohibit mobile phone usage during instructional periods."
